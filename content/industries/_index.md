Sector pages usually claim everything. This one separates two things a buyer
should be able to tell apart: sectors where work has been delivered, and sectors
where the problem shape is familiar and the depth is transferred from adjacent
work.

| Sector | Position |
|---|---|
| [Energy and utilities](/industries/energy-and-utilities/) | Delivered. Active engagement in grid and infrastructure spatial analysis |
| [Manufacturing and logistics](/industries/manufacturing-and-logistics/) | Transferred. Operations automation patterns, no named engagement in the sector yet |
| [Financial services](/industries/financial-services/) | Transferred. Regulated architecture and residency work, no named engagement in the sector yet |
| [Public sector](/industries/public-sector/) | Adjacent. Publicly funded research and governance work rather than a delivered public authority project |

Saying this costs a few enquiries. It costs far less than being found out in the
second meeting.

## What actually transfers between sectors

More than the sector pages of most consultancies imply. The four recurring problem
shapes on this site, agents and process automation, document and knowledge
analysis, geospatial analysis, and media pipelines, are not sector-specific. A
verifiable-selection pattern built for video applies directly to contract
extraction. A hybrid split designed for grid data applies to patient data.

What does not transfer is domain knowledge: the regulation, the vocabulary, and
what a plausible answer looks like to an expert. That comes from your side of the
table, which is why the workshop format puts your domain experts in the room
rather than interviewing them afterwards.

## The regulated common denominator

Energy, finance, healthcare and the public sector share one constraint: some data
cannot sit in a public region, and the architecture has to be designed around that
rather than apologise for it. That position is on
[Hybrid and open source](/platforms/hybrid-and-open-source/), and it is the same
answer in all four.
