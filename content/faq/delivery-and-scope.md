## How long does a typical engagement take?

Depends on the model. A workshop series is one day or two to three sessions across
a few weeks. A proof of concept is 4 to 10 weeks. An architecture audit is 2 to 4
weeks. A production phase is 4 to 12 weeks. Fractional leadership and retainers
run in months. See [Engagement models](/engagements/).

## Who does the work, and does that change after signing?

The named lead in the proposal does the work or directs it, and does not change
without your agreement. Specialists come from the
[associate network](/team/network/), are named in the proposal, and are introduced
before they start.

## How big is the team?

Smaller than you expect. Most projects need one or two senior people. A workshop
series is one facilitator. A proof of concept is usually one builder with a second
pair of eyes on architecture and security. If a proposal has five names on it, ask
what each of them is for.

## What if the scope moves?

It usually does, because discovery finds things. The mechanism: a scope change is
written down, priced, and agreed before the work continues. No silent absorption,
which quietly damages the engagement, and no change-request theatre over an
afternoon's work.

Fixed-fee work moves only when the scope moves, and the assumptions the fee rests
on are listed in the statement of work so it is clear when one has been broken.

## What do you need from us?

Three things, and the third is the one projects fail on:

1. A decision maker who can say yes.
2. Access to the data, or a written explanation of why it is not available. The
   second is a legitimate answer and changes the plan.
3. One person from your side who is genuinely available during the build. Not
   available in principle. Available.

## What does the weekly rhythm look like?

Something running to look at, every week, plus a short written note of what was
decided. No month-long silences and no big reveal at the end. Decisions get
recorded with their alternatives so they can be reviewed by someone who was not in
the room.

## Do you work on site or remotely?

Both. Workshops in Vienna and the surrounding region are usually on site, because a
room full of the right people is worth the travel. Build phases are usually remote,
because they are not improved by it. Travel beyond Vienna is agreed in advance.

## Who owns the code?

You do. It is in your repository from the first commit, not handed over at the
end. Where a pattern or component of ours is used, the terms that let you keep
using and modifying it are in the statement of work rather than discovered later.

## What happens at the end?

A [handover](/method/handover/) phase: documentation, decision records, a runbook,
a recorded walkthrough with your team, and an ownership map. Credentials are
revoked on the last day and that is confirmed in writing. An optional support tail
is bounded and has its end date in the agreement.

## What do you turn down?

- Staff augmentation and unmanaged offshore delivery
- Work where nobody will commit to a success metric
- Projects where the data required does not exist and nobody wants to hear that
- Anything requiring a discipline nobody in the network holds. That gets a
  referral instead of a bluff
- Engagements with no capacity to do properly. We say so and offer a date

## What if it goes wrong?

You hear it early, in writing, with options. The most common version is not a
technical failure but a discovery that the original premise was wrong, which is
what the kill criteria and the phase gates exist to surface while there is still
budget to redirect.
